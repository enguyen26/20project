Paris Travel Guide

Île-de-France, France • Updated January 2025

Paris needs no introduction, yet it somehow exceeds expectations every single time. Beyond the Eiffel Tower and the Louvre, there's a city that reveals itself slowly—through morning croissants at corner boulangeries, late afternoons in hidden parks, and evenings that stretch into the early hours at wine bars tucked down cobblestone streets.

The key to Paris isn't rushing from landmark to landmark. It's wandering aimlessly through Le Marais, discovering a century-old bookshop, or spending an entire afternoon at a café watching the city move around you. This guide focuses on the Paris that locals know: the neighborhoods worth exploring, the restaurants where you'll want to linger, and the experiences that make you understand why people never stop coming back.

Where to Stay in Paris

Le Marais is ideal if you want to be in the heart of everything. The neighborhood blends medieval architecture with modern boutiques, vintage shops, and some of the city's best falafel. It's walkable to major sites but feels residential enough that you're living like a local.

Saint-Germain-des-Prés on the Left Bank offers classic Parisian elegance. This is the Paris of literary cafés, art galleries, and the Luxembourg Gardens. It's quieter than Le Marais but equally central, with better access to the Latin Quarter and Musée d'Orsay.

Canal Saint-Martin is where younger Parisians actually hang out. The canal-side area has a laid-back vibe, excellent brunch spots, and interesting bars. It's a bit further from major tourist sites, but the metro makes everything accessible, and you'll appreciate having a neighborhood that feels authentic.

For budget stays, look at Belleville or the 11th arrondissement. Both have good metro connections, affordable accommodations, and genuine neighborhood character without the tourist crowds.

Where to Eat

Skip the restaurants with photos on the menu near major tourist sites. The best meals in Paris happen at neighborhood bistros where menus change with the seasons and locals fill every table.

For breakfast: Find the nearest boulangerie. A pain au chocolat and café crème at the counter costs less than €5 and beats any hotel breakfast. Bakeries like Du Pain et des Idées in the 10th or Mamiche in the 9th are worth seeking out specifically.

For lunch: Many restaurants offer fixed-price lunch menus (formules) that are significantly cheaper than dinner. This is when you should splurge on nicer places. Try Frenchie Bar à Vins for small plates, or Ellsworth for modern bistro cooking that doesn't take itself too seriously.

For dinner: Book ahead for popular spots like Septime or Le Chateaubriand, or embrace spontaneity at wine bars like Le Verre Volé where you can eat excellent cheese and charcuterie without a reservation. The natural wine scene in Paris is exceptional—don't be afraid to ask for recommendations.

For coffee: Paris is finally embracing specialty coffee. Belleville Brûlerie, Café Lomi, and Boot Café serve espresso that rivals Melbourne or Portland, using beans roasted locally.

What to Do

Start with the obvious: Yes, go to the Eiffel Tower, but skip the lines and view it from Trocadéro at sunset instead. Visit the Louvre, but have a plan—three hours is enough to see highlights without exhausting yourself. The Musée d'Orsay is equally impressive and less overwhelming, housed in a stunning former railway station.

Walk everywhere you can. Paris reveals itself on foot. Cross the Seine on different bridges. Wander through the covered passages (Galerie Vivienne is particularly beautiful). Get lost in the Latin Quarter's bookshops and the Île de la Cité's quiet corners.

Visit a market. Marché Bastille (Thursday and Sunday) has everything from fresh produce to vintage clothing. Marché des Enfants Rouges in Le Marais is Paris's oldest covered market, perfect for lunch from one of the international food stalls.

See Paris from above. The Eiffel Tower is fine, but the view from Montparnasse Tower is better (and includes the Eiffel Tower in the skyline). Or climb to Sacré-Cœur in Montmartre for free sunset views over the city.

My Favorite Spots

  • Jardin du Luxembourg for an afternoon with a book
  • Promenade Plantée for a quiet walk on an elevated park
  • Shakespeare and Company for browsing English books
  • Merci concept store in Le Marais for design inspiration
  • Musée Rodin for sculpture gardens without the Louvre crowds
  • Le Bon Marché food hall for edible souvenirs
  • Parc des Buttes-Chaumont for sunset views away from tourists
  • Rue des Martyrs for food shopping like a local

Practical Tips

Transportation: Buy a carnet of 10 metro tickets or get a Navigo pass if staying longer than a few days. The metro is efficient and reaches everywhere you need to go. Walking between stops is often just as fast and more enjoyable.

Money: Cards are widely accepted, but keep some cash for smaller cafés and markets. Tipping isn't required—service is included in your bill.

Language: Learning basic French pleasantries (bonjour, merci, s'il vous plaît) goes a long way. Many Parisians speak English, especially in tourist areas, but attempting French first is appreciated.

Timing: Visit in spring (April-May) or fall (September-October) for pleasant weather and fewer crowds. August sees many locals on vacation and some restaurants closed, but the city is quieter and parking spots actually exist.

Museums: Many museums offer free admission on the first Sunday of each month. Book Louvre tickets online in advance and enter through the Porte des Lions entrance to avoid main entrance lines.

Safety: Paris is generally safe, but watch for pickpockets in tourist areas and on the metro. The usual city awareness applies—don't leave bags unattended, watch your phone on packed trains.
